Most local businesses in Nepal do not need a website because they are "already on Facebook" — or so the thinking goes. But a social profile is a rented space. The algorithm decides who sees it, the platform can change the rules overnight, and the page belongs to the platform, not to you.

A website is different. It is a place you own, on a domain you control, that works the same way whether someone found you on Google, came from a link a friend sent, or typed your name into a browser.

## What a website actually does

A good small-business website does three things, and nothing else:

1. **It explains what you do**, in your own words, without a character limit.
2. **It makes it easy to contact you**, with a path that works for the person asking — phone, WhatsApp, or a form.
3. **It earns trust**, because a real site shows up in search results, carries your hours and address, and stays the same from one year to the next.

None of these require a big budget. They require a decision to have a site at all.

## The gap is wider than it looks

Here is the part that surprises business owners: the customers they are losing are not looking for a website. They are looking for the thing a website provides.

Someone in Kathmandu searches for "mithai shop near New Road." The results show shops with sites, hours listed, menus online, and directions. Your shop — the one with the better sweets and the longer family history — is not there, because there is nothing for Google to show.

That is not a failure of marketing. It is a failure of being findable. And being findable is the cheapest part of running a business online.

## What it costs, and what it doesn't

A website is not a one-time purchase you forget about, and it is not a monthly subscription to a platform you do not control. It is a small, durable asset: a set of pages that load fast, work on a phone, and can be updated when your hours change or a new product arrives.
